# Growth, Limits and Perceptions

## What I Want to Grow In
1. Building end-to-end AI solutions. I’ve worked with data science, machine learning, generative AI, RAG and agents. I want to go further: take an idea from the business problem through architecture, deployment, evaluation, optimisation and monitoring into a reliable product, independently.
2. Leadership and decision-making. I enjoy working with people and bringing different views together. I want to own larger projects, guide a team, talk with senior stakeholders and make good decisions when things are uncertain.
3. Business and product thinking. I want to strengthen the mix of technical and management views, asking not only whether something is possible, but whether it’s valuable, scalable and solves the customer’s real problem.

## The Harmless Misconception
People who don’t know me well sometimes think I’m very serious, purely technical, or someone who prefers to work alone, because I get very focused on hard problems. Once they know me, they find out I’m funny, talkative and love making people laugh. I have two modes: logical and focused when solving problems, light and fun socially. Work should be productive, but that doesn’t mean the environment has to be stressful.

## How I Push My Boundaries and Limits
I pick up problems I don’t yet know how to solve. A recent example is a project to build a RAG-based AI agent that understands a large Java codebase and helps find and fix bugs. It did not go to completion: after a while the cost made it impractical, and tools that already do this exist in the market, so I stopped. 

The challenge was that cutting Java files into random chunks loses the relationships between classes and methods. I learned new ideas for me: Abstract Syntax Trees, Tree-sitter, code-aware chunking, embeddings (E5), FAISS for search, and MongoDB for storing code embeddings. When it kept pulling the wrong code, I didn’t just swap the model; I investigated the retrieval step to see where it went wrong. What I learned is that I don’t need to know everything before I start. I need to be willing to learn, experiment, make mistakes and keep improving. It also taught me when to stop: when the cost didn’t make sense and good tools already existed in the market, I chose not to keep building it.
