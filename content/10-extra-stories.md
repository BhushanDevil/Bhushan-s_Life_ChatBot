## A Mistake or Failure


One thing I have learned throughout my journey is that making mistakes is not the problem. Repeating the same mistake without learning from it is.

I remember working on a data analytics requirement at FCI CCM where my initial approach was to focus primarily on getting the correct output from the SQL query.

I was able to retrieve the required information, but when the query was used for a larger dataset and connected to the dashboard, performance became a challenge. Some queries were taking several minutes, and certain operations were timing out.

Initially, I was trying to solve the problem by modifying the query itself without fully considering the underlying data-processing approach.

Eventually, I realised that I was focusing too much on getting the result and not enough on how efficiently the result was being generated.

I stepped back, examined the joins, filters, and aggregations, and started thinking about the actual information the business needed rather than retrieving every available record.

That experience changed my approach to problem-solving.

Today, before implementing something, I try to understand three things: whether the solution is correct, whether it is efficient, and whether it will scale when the data grows.

I don't consider that experience a failure in the traditional sense. I consider it an important learning moment because it changed how I approach technical problems.

My biggest takeaway was that getting something to work is good, but understanding how to make it work properly is what makes you better.
## Teamwork or Disagreement
I believe teamwork is not about everyone agreeing with each other. It is about everyone working towards the same objective, even when they have different opinions.

One example of a situation I could use is a disagreement around how to approach a reporting requirement.

Imagine a situation where one team member prefers retrieving detailed raw records to maintain flexibility, while another prefers building aggregated datasets to improve dashboard performance.

Both approaches have advantages, and instead of arguing about which approach is better, I believe the first step is to understand what the business actually needs.

My approach would be to bring the discussion back to the objective. Do stakeholders need individual-level information, or do they primarily need summarized business metrics? What are the performance requirements? How frequently does the data need to be refreshed?

Once those questions are clear, we can compare the approaches and choose one based on evidence rather than personal preference.

I generally try to listen to everyone's perspective before sharing my own opinion.

I also don't believe in taking technical disagreements personally. Someone challenging my idea doesn't mean they are challenging me as a person.

I would rather have a team member point out a weakness in my solution before deployment than have the entire team face a problem later.

For me, good teamwork means respecting people, communicating openly, sharing responsibility, and keeping the common goal bigger than individual opinions.

## Handling Pressure and Tight Deadlines
I generally handle pressure by trying to bring structure to the situation rather than allowing the pressure to control my thinking.

In my experience at FCI CCM, there were reporting requirements involving large datasets, multiple tables, complex SQL queries, and dashboard expectations.

Sometimes, the challenge was not just completing the work but ensuring that the output was accurate and useful within the available time.

My approach is to divide the problem into smaller parts.

First, I identify what is most important and what the final business requirement actually is. Then I break the work into smaller tasks, prioritise the critical components, and identify potential blockers early.

For example, when working on analytical reporting, I would focus first on validating the source data and ensuring the core metrics were correct. After that, I would work on the query logic, aggregations, dashboard presentation, and final validation.

I also believe communication is extremely important under pressure. If something is going to take longer than expected, I prefer communicating the issue early rather than waiting until the deadline to explain it.

Another thing that helps me is maintaining a calm mindset.

I believe that panicking rarely improves a situation. Whether the problem is technical, professional, or personal, I try to separate what is urgent from what is important and focus on the next actionable step.

I am not saying that I never feel pressure. Of course, I do. But I have learned not to let that pressure affect the quality of my decisions.

**My philosophy is simple: when the pressure increases, I try to become more organised, more focused, and more logical rather than more stressed.**
